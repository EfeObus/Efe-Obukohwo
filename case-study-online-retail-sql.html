Online Retail SQL Analysis

PostgreSQL Database Design & Advanced Business Intelligence

Dec 2010 – Dec 2011 Data 500,000+ Transactions 30 Production SQL Queries

Project Overview

A complete SQL analytics project built on the UCI Online Retail dataset — 500,000+ line-item transactions from a UK-based online gift and homeware wholesaler. I designed a normalized PostgreSQL schema from the raw CSV export, then wrote 30 production-ready queries covering customer segmentation, product performance, temporal trends, and anomaly detection using window functions, CTEs, and statistical aggregation. The goal wasn't just to produce numbers — it was to produce numbers I could defend, which meant auditing my own query outputs for the kind of quiet mistakes that make analysis wrong without looking wrong.

The Data

Each row in the raw export is one product line on one invoice, not one "purchase" — a single customer order typically spans many rows. The dataset covers December 1, 2010 through December 9, 2011, and reads like a wholesale gift catalog: lanterns, ceramic jars, novelty stationery, with order sizes ranging from single units to an 80,995-unit bulk order of the same SKU. A large share of customers are wholesalers buying to resell, not retail consumers — which shapes almost every downstream finding.

Why This Dataset Makes a Real SQL Portfolio Piece

It has the properties that make analytics work hard in practice: ~25% of rows carry no CustomerID, returns need correct sign handling, non-product rows (manual adjustments, postage) are mixed into the fact table, and revenue follows a genuinely skewed long-tail distribution rather than a clean synthetic curve. Queries that look right on a toy dataset need real adjustment here.

Database Design

Normalized the raw single-table CSV export into a proper relational schema before writing a single analytical query:

  • 5 core tables + 1 staging table: countries (38 rows), customers (4,372), products (4,070), invoices (22,190), invoice_details (397,924 line items), plus a staging_raw_import table holding the untouched 500,000-row source for traceability back to the original export.
  • 3NF with surrogate keys: SERIAL primary keys and indexed foreign keys across the fact/dimension relationships, rather than querying the flat CSV structure directly.
  • Correct type handling: DECIMAL/NUMERIC casting throughout so currency aggregation doesn't accumulate floating-point drift across hundreds of thousands of rows.

Query Techniques

30 queries organized into customer analytics, product analysis, temporal trends, and advanced analytics, built with:

  • Window functions: RANK() for product/customer leaderboards, LAG() for month-over-month growth, SUM() OVER (PARTITION BY...) for running totals, NTILE() for LTV quartiles, PERCENTILE_CONT() for top-1% transaction thresholds
  • CTEs: multi-step logic for RFM segmentation, cohort retention, and a Herfindahl-style product diversity index
  • Statistical functions: STDDEV-based anomaly detection for unusual order patterns
-- Cumulative revenue by country (running total via window function)
WITH daily_country_revenue AS (
  SELECT DATE(i.invoice_date) AS sale_date,
         co.country_name,
         ROUND(SUM(id.line_total)::NUMERIC, 2) AS daily_revenue
  FROM invoices i
  JOIN invoice_details id ON i.invoice_id = id.invoice_id
  JOIN customers c ON i.customer_id = c.customer_id
  JOIN countries co ON c.country_id = co.country_id
  GROUP BY DATE(i.invoice_date), co.country_name
)
SELECT sale_date, country_name, daily_revenue,
       SUM(daily_revenue) OVER (
         PARTITION BY country_name ORDER BY sale_date
       ) AS cumulative_revenue
FROM daily_country_revenue
ORDER BY country_name, sale_date;

  • Revenue concentration is moderate, not extreme: the top 10% of customers by lifetime value drive 61% of revenue — closer to a 60/10 split than the textbook 80/20, and the single highest-value customer (Netherlands, £280,206 across 74 orders) more than doubles the #2 account.
  • It's a volume business, not a margin business: budget products under £5 make up 2,854 of 3,665 products sold and 71% of total revenue, while luxury items (£25+) are only 41 products and 2.8% of revenue.
  • The UK isn't the largest market — it's nearly the entire market: 82% of revenue from 3,921 of 4,339 customers. Netherlands is #2 by revenue share (3.2%) with only 9 customers, implying large wholesale order values rather than a broad consumer base.
  • No Saturday transactions exist anywhere in the dataset — not low weekend volume, an actual gap consistent with the warehouse being closed Saturdays. Thursday is the highest-revenue day (£1.98M); 12:00–13:00 is the single highest-revenue hour.
  • November 2011 is the clear seasonal peak (2,658 transactions, £1.16M) — consistent with pre-holiday wholesale buying, with month-over-month revenue swinging between -21% and +48% in complete months.

Auditing My Own Analysis

Running a query and getting a result isn't the same as the result being right. Rather than stopping at "the queries ran successfully," I inspected the actual exported outputs against the raw data and found three real problems in my own analysis:

The CURRENT_DATE Bug

Queries classifying customers as Active/At Risk/Dormant compared purchase dates to CURRENT_DATE. Since the dataset ends in December 2011, every customer's "days since last purchase" is now 5,000+ days — so the recency segmentation showed 100% of customers as "Inactive," carrying zero information as written. The fix: anchor recency to (SELECT MAX(invoice_date) FROM invoices) instead of today's date.

The Cohort Query That Only Returned Month Zero

The cohort retention query was designed to show a month-1/2/3/6+ retention curve. The actual export had exactly 13 rows — one per cohort month, all at months_since_first_purchase = 0. The retention curve the query was built to produce never appeared, a real bug worth catching before quoting a "70% retention by month 3" number that the query never actually computed.

An Anomaly Filter Too Permissive to Be Useful

The STDDEV-based anomaly detection query flagged 3,396 of 3,754 customers (90%) as "High Volume Spikes" at a 1.5 standard deviation threshold — too loose to function as an outlier filter on this data. Documented as a known limitation with the fix (tightening to 2–3 STDDEV) rather than shipping a misleading "anomaly" label on 90% of customers.

Also documented: ~25% of raw rows have no CustomerID and are silently excluded from every customer-level query (meaning customer-grouped revenue always undercounts true activity), and December 2011 is a partial month (9 days) that produces a misleading -55% month-over-month "decline" if not excluded from seasonal comparisons.
